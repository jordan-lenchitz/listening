# listening to *a cappella* singing: an ecological approach

# tl;dr
listeners who have [learned to take up their affordance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibsonian_ecological_theory_of_development) perceive certain [pitches of spectral fission](https://youtu.be/m1SKJ5ZA5HI?si=vIj75nll8JCKxYak&t=100). multi-pitch trackers tend to find these only as a side effect which motivates my addition of an [affordance field](https://github.com/jordan-lenchitz/listening/blob/main/affordance_blurb.md) showing where their perception is supported by the sound itself

# full abstract
This dissertation considers how listeners to _a cappella_ singing can hear pitches that arise not from any single vocal line but from the acoustic interactions between voices and the interpretive frameworks of those who hear them. Often described as “phantom tones,” “ghost voices,” or “the voice of the angels,” these emergent phenomena challenge the presumed boundaries between timbre and pitch and between the produced and the perceived. I examine this phenomenon across four vocal traditions: American barbershop, Sardinian _cuncordu_, Michael Harrison’s _Just Constellations_ (2016), and Toby Twining’s _Chrysalid Requiem_ (1995-2001). Drawing on the acoustics of the human voice, the psychophysics of auditory perception, and auditory scene analysis, I develop mathematical and computational models to trace how these sounds emerge from specific configurations of tuning, timbre, and vocal formant alignment. These models are grounded and tested through ethnographic fieldwork, a controlled listening study, and sustained attention to how performers and non-performers alike describe their own listening experiences. Rather than treating listening as the passive registration of sonic data, I approach it phenomenologically as an embodied, interpretive, and often socially conditioned practice. I argue that pitch perception, especially in contexts involving spectral fission or periodicity pitch, can be productively understood in terms of affordance: listeners do not merely detect frequencies but are invited, by the structure of the sound and their own learned attentional habits, to hear certain frequencies as stable, salient, or meaningful. Statements like “listeners tend to hear what they expect to hear” are from this viewpoint not tautologies but crucial starting points for theorizing the contingency of musical experience. This dissertation offers both analytical tools and conceptual vocabularies for theorizing listening as a dynamic and plural process that resists reduction to fixed structural models and instead foregrounds how musical sounds take shape in perception. Ultimately, I argue that _a cappella_ singing is a rich terrain for reconsidering the relationship between sound and the act of listening.
